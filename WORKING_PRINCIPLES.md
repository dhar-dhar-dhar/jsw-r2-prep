# Working principles for investment research (merged, 3 Oct 2026)

Merged from the Titan assignment (this repo) and two other sessions' lists. Titan-specific mechanics are cut; what
remains transfers to any research, thesis or portfolio job. Read before starting a new assignment.

## 1. What the work is for
1. Form a point of view. The output presents a synthesised view built from the analysis. It never walks the reader
   through the workbook, and the reader never has to assemble facts into a conclusion.
2. Cohesion comes first. A cold reader goes straight through: structure, connecting tissue, clean hygiene.
3. Every word earns its place. More data is not more insight. A well-backed number dump still fails.
4. Answer the exact question, in its shape: every part of it, its constraints ("one way forward, don't hedge"), its
   emphasis. One recommendation, no blends of options.
5. Decision first. Lead with the decision, then the deciding numbers, then the condition and the first risk. Each
   headline makes sense alone, with the mechanism underneath.
6. Think from the investor's seat. Say what the fund gets back, at what value, by when and by what route. Never
   assume rights or facts you cannot show: board seats, cheque sizes, holdings.

## 2. How a section reads
7. Lead with the so-what, then the evidence that earns it. A section opens with its answer, readable alone.
8. The answer lines, read alone and in order, carry the whole argument (the spine test).
9. Handovers between sections give a reason, never a signpost.
10. Define every term where it first appears. Never assume the reader opens the annex, workbook or glossary.
11. One point per sentence. Split anything a reader would read twice. One number makes the point; the rest sits in
    the exhibit.
12. One name for one thing throughout: one loss measure, one term per fund, one set of scenario names.
13. Fact, inference and decision threshold are kept apart on every page. Inference is never written as fact.

## 3. Analytical standards
14. Read like an investor, not an accountant. Five questions: is revenue real, does each sale make money, how fast is
    it burning, where does the money come from, what is weird.
15. Write the tests before opening the data. Every option is an assumption in disguise: name the number that kills it.
16. Tests must be able to fail. Pass or fail thresholds on every condition. A missing document is not a failed thesis.
17. Show the reasoning: mechanism, counterfactual, a precedent that tests it, and the weak point.
18. Compare like with like. One metric definition, matching periods, the same treatment on both sides, a category
    against a category. Flag different bases instead of scoring across them.
19. Separate one-off swings from structural problems. The worst month is not typical, and a fix that only reverses
    last month is not a fix.
20. Test every rule or trigger against history before recommending it.
21. Quantify the alternatives the decision rests on: expand now vs later, sell now vs wait, switch vs stay.
22. Asymmetric effort: judgment goes where it can kill the case, not spread thin.
23. Events after the data cut-off must be reconciled with the verdict. Check whether management is already moving
    against the recommendation, and address it.
24. Recommendations must be enforceable. Say who acts, through what lever, and what they cannot force. Where the
    investor's entities have different interests, say which way the trade-off falls.
25. Attack your own thesis. State the strongest counterargument, what would reverse the view, the first questions a
    partner would ask with their defences, and the questions only the company can answer.
26. Comps are per company against its own competitors, tiered. Never portfolio companies side by side.
27. Bottom-up sizing (persona, population, funnel, order value). Top-down only as a labelled fallback.

## 4. Evidence and figures
28. Source every number: MIS sheet and row, or publisher, date and URL. If it cannot be found, say so instead of
    estimating. Every "not found" gets a proxy hunt.
29. Primary data beats consultant reports and aggregators. Aggregator figures need checking.
30. Tag confidence on every claim (H/M/L, stale) and label the claim type: fact, management claim, publicly reported
    term, inference or assumption.
31. Assumptions only when backed by data, logic and a stated basis. Never a multiple or timeline out of thin air.
32. Compute precisely: unrounded inputs, multiples to two decimals, IRR on exact dates, one convention for every
    investment.
33. Never change a verified figure silently. Stop, flag it, and log the ruling.
34. Where stakes or prices are not public, show returns per Rs 1 invested under stated cases.
35. State causation only as strongly as the evidence allows. Name each data gap once, then turn it into a diligence
    question.

## 5. Working models
36. Fact base first, argument second. A fact enters only if it helps the decision.
37. Workbooks are working models: live formulas off visible inputs, raw inputs the only typed numbers with sources
    beside them, no units in cells, no internal notes. A pasted figure, even a correct one, fails.
38. Edit by script on the source, never by hand in the output file. Gate every build.
39. The workbook holds the depth. The note holds the synthesis.

## 6. Output and presentation
40. Verdict visible at a glance: decision, deciding number, conditions and risks on the front page. Length is fine
    if the front carries everything. Core separate from the annex, with real page limits.
41. Investment-bank quality: one colour code, one font hierarchy, a source line under every exhibit, no oversized
    cells, no near-empty pages. It must not look machine-made.
42. Formal voice, one author. No dashes, semicolons, contractions, hype or slop, including in labels and headings.
43. Nothing internal survives a submission: no correction marks, no "we", no invented owners or commitments, zero
    contradictions.
44. Final QA always: citations, period labels, audited vs unaudited, index page numbers. Open and save Excel before
    sending. Version names aligned across note, annex and workbook.
45. Confidentiality: case data never goes into web searches or external services. Notes from other deals never mix
    into a case repo.

## 7. Process
46. Fix the source data before the documents built on it: corrections, then workbook, then briefs, then drafts.
47. Insights before drafting. List the insights from the analysis, get them approved or take the call when delegated,
    then write from them. Plan the structure first: a blueprint per section and an argument chain per company.
48. AI as tutor and skeptic, never ghostwriter. AI generates the questions; the data answers them. Your own
    scratchpad first, the parallel pass second, then diff the two.
49. Cold-read audits by a reader who sees only the final output. Independent reviews merged into one actionable file,
    marked must-fix vs safe to ignore.
50. Bring decisions with a recommendation; Kushal decides. When asked for a fix, give replacement text to paste.
51. Hand heavy work to agents without lowering the bar. Strong models for judgment and writing, faster models for
    volume, independent auditors for checks. Watch usage limits and use fresh sessions for long jobs.
52. Push every output three or four times past what any AI-using candidate would submit.
53. Commit and push often. Report outcomes plainly, including what was not done.
